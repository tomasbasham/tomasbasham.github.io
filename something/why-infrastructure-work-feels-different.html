Why Infrastructure Work Feels Different

Blog Logo

Tomas Basham

on 09 Sep 2026
About 5 min read

I’m an application developer by trade. I ended up working in infrastructure and platform engineering out of necessity - someone had to run the Kubernetes cluster, someone had to own the cloud account - and somewhere along the way it became the work I love most. It took me a long time to work out why.

The obvious explanation, and why it’s wrong

The easy answer is that infrastructure has fewer valid ways to do something. Networking works a particular way. The Linux kernel works a particular way. Stray from the accepted approach and things simply stop working. Application development, by contrast, seems to have too many opinions - endless ways to skin the same cat, and teams that spend real time arguing about which way is correct.

I believed this for a while, and it’s half right at best. Infrastructure isn’t actually short of opinions. Ask five platform engineers how to structure a multi-tenant Kubernetes cluster, whether to run a service mesh, how secrets should be managed, or what your GitOps topology should look like, and you’ll get five confidently different answers - argued with exactly the same intensity as any application development debate about state management or dependency injection.

So “fewer opinions” doesn’t hold up. Something else is going on.

The actual explanation

The real difference isn’t the number of valid designs. It’s that the failure mode is legible.

Get a BGP configuration wrong, and routes don’t propagate. Get kernel cgroup settings wrong, and the container gets OOM-killed. The system tells you, unambiguously and quickly, that you were wrong. There’s no room for the failure to be argued away, reframed, or attributed to taste.

A bad abstraction in an application codebase doesn’t page anyone at 3am. It doesn’t fail loudly. It just slowly makes the codebase worse, in a way that’s contestable - someone can always argue it’s fine, or that the real problem is somewhere else, or that it’s a matter of preference. Correctness in application architecture is often adjudicated by consensus, because there’s no kernel standing over the code ready to reject it.

What I actually value, then, isn’t a smaller solution space. It’s fast, harsh, non-negotiable feedback. The system either works or it doesn’t, and it tells you which almost immediately.

Domain modelling that’s already done for you

I tend to approach architecture through domain modelling first - trying to understand the shape of the problem before writing anything. I think this is a large part of why infrastructure work resonates so strongly with me specifically.

In infrastructure, the domain model is very often already correct before I arrive. The model for how a TCP handshake works isn’t up for negotiation. Someone solved that decades ago, rigorously, and it’s simply true. My job is to discover and apply that model correctly, not to invent it.

In application development, I’m frequently the one who has to invent the domain model - extracted from a messy, ambiguous, and often contested business reality. And once I’ve built that model, other people get a vote on whether it’s right, because there’s no external authority to appeal to. One of these is discovery. The other is invention under scrutiny. They are very different postures to hold day to day, and I know which one I find more restful.

Why infrastructure work is easier to do alone

There’s a related observation worth separating out, because it looks like the same claim but isn’t: infrastructure work is often easier to solo. The task is clear, and the solution is often just as clear, or more so.

It would be tempting to say this is because the problems are simpler. They’re not. It’s because the blast radius of a decision is usually bounded and observable. I can make a call, ship it, and watch metrics tell me within minutes or hours whether I was right - without first needing four other people to sign off. Application architecture decisions frequently can’t be validated that fast; the consequences of a wrong choice surface weeks or months later, diffused across a codebase multiple people touch. When feedback is slow, consensus becomes a substitute for it - you get agreement up front because you can’t get proof. Slow feedback loops breed committees. Fast feedback loops let one competent person simply get on with it.

The prediction this makes - and where it holds

If this theory is right, it makes a testable prediction: the parts of platform work that are most application-development-flavoured should feel less satisfying than the deep infrastructure layer, because that’s exactly where the fast, legible feedback disappears again. Designing the API for an internal developer platform, or deciding whether something should be a CRD or a Helm chart, has no protocol standing behind it ready to say “wrong.” It’s a design decision, adjudicated by taste and consensus, just like application architecture.

That prediction holds for me. I enjoy that layer of work too - but noticeably less than the deep infrastructure layer beneath it. Which suggests the feedback-loop explanation is doing the real work here, not the “fewer opinions” explanation I started with.

The conclusion

Infrastructure work rewards being right quickly rather than persuasive slowly. The domain is already correctly modelled by physics and protocol, so the work is discovery rather than invention-under-scrutiny. And because the blast radius of most decisions is bounded and observable, it’s possible to act alone and let the system itself settle arguments that would otherwise need a committee.

That’s a satisfying way to spend a working day. It’s also, as it turns out, exactly the wrong way to spend the working days before the work begins - which is a different problem, and one I’ll get to separately.
