The WIP Limit Isn't the Problem

Blog Logo

Tomas Basham

on 11 Sep 2026
About 7 min read

In an earlier post I described a mismatch: I want slow, deliberate, many-voiced feedback before work starts, and fast, unambiguous, solitary feedback once it does. The resolution was that these aren’t contradictory preferences — they’re the same principle, matching the feedback mechanism to whichever oracle can actually answer the question at that stage. Human judgement for direction. The system for execution.

There’s a related tension I’ve watched play out repeatedly on a small team, and it doesn’t resolve the same way. It looks, on the surface, like a scheduling problem. It isn’t.

The oscillation

The pattern goes roughly like this. The team takes on too many workstreams at once — not because anyone mis-scoped the sprint, but because everybody wants something of their own to work on, or believes their thing is the higher priority, despite that not really being their call to make. So the sprint ends up planned as a mixture, a bit of everything, to keep everyone satisfied going in.

Inevitably, little of it finishes cleanly. People are pulled out of their own work constantly — into code review, into testing sessions, into implementation discussions for streams they’re not even driving — and by the end of the sprint nobody has actually finished anything. What does get finished is rushed across the line in the last day or two.

So the retrospective conclusion is sensible: focus on less. Reduce WIP. The next sprint, people pair or mob rather than each running their own stream.

And then a different problem shows up. Only one person can drive at a time. The others — the navigators — get bored. They start quietly working on side things during the session, less engaged, present but not really there. The work does get finished on time this time. But people feel flat about it. Demotivated. Under-stretched. Convinced more could have been achieved.

So the next retrospective says: let’s take on more, give people their own things again. And the cycle restarts.

Why this isn’t a WIP problem

The obvious read is that the team hasn’t found the right WIP limit yet — too high causes thrashing, too low causes disengagement, so somewhere in between must be correct. I don’t think that’s right, and there’s a tell in the pattern that gives it away: it’s frequently the same people, unhappy in both states. The person complaining “I never get to finish anything, everything’s rushed” in the high-WIP sprint is often the same person complaining “I’m bored, I’m not really contributing” in the paired sprint.

If WIP were the actual variable, you wouldn’t expect that. You’d expect the complaints to come from different people depending on their tolerance for context-switching versus their tolerance for close collaboration. Instead the same individuals are dissatisfied on both sides of the dial, which suggests the dial isn’t what they actually care about.

What they’re asking for, in both states, looks like the same underlying thing: individual, attributable ownership of a solved problem. Something they drove, that’s recognisably theirs, that demonstrates the specific expertise they bring to the team. High WIP gives everyone a shot at that — their own stream, their own area — but not the completion of it, because constant interruption means nothing actually gets finished under their name. Pairing gives the completion, but only to whoever’s driving that session; the navigator gets the outcome without the authorship.

Neither format is failing at its stated job, either. Throughput-wise, the low-WIP sprints work — the team said as much, work gets done on time. What’s failing is a need nobody’s named out loud in the retrospective, because “reduce WIP” and “pair more” are the only vocabulary a sprint retrospective tends to offer. Recognition and demonstrated individual expertise aren’t on that menu, so the dissatisfaction keeps getting misdiagnosed as a process setting, and the process gets nudged back and forth in search of a value that isn’t on that axis at all.

It’s the same shape of mistake as the oracle mismatch from the earlier post, one level up: using a process mechanism — a WIP limit, a pairing cadence — to solve a need that mechanism was never built to answer. A WIP limit is a good tool for managing flow efficiency and reducing thrash. It has no lever for felt ownership. Turning the dial harder in either direction won’t produce a value it was never designed to hold.

Where “shaping” fits, and where it doesn’t yet

This team has already tried, independently, to address a version of this — worth mentioning, because it’s evidence the diagnosis isn’t invented after the fact. We’ve been experimenting with a shaping model rather than conventional planning: a rotating pair kicks off each initiative with a lightweight outline, the relevant specialists feed in asynchronously by area, a short huddle resolves anything unclear, and the facilitator role — whoever drives that shaping — rotates each time rather than defaulting to the same person.

The intent lines up closely with the diagnosis above. Rotating the facilitator role exists specifically so ownership of direction doesn’t collapse onto one person, and so people get turns leading rather than only ever being told what to build by some fixed authority. That’s a genuine, structural answer to part of the ownership problem — at the design stage.

But it’s a partial answer, not a full one, because it operates entirely upstream of the point where the oscillation actually happens. Shaping answers “who got heard on the plan, and who’s facilitating it.” It doesn’t yet answer “who gets to drive — and finish, under their own name — the implementation.” A team can rotate shaping facilitators well and still hit the exact same pairing-versus-solo tension once building starts, because that tension lives entirely in execution, which shaping doesn’t touch. The gap this post is describing sits downstream of everything shaping currently covers.

What would actually close the gap

If the diagnosis is right, the fix isn’t a better WIP number, and it isn’t more or less pairing as a blanket policy. It’s treating ownership itself as something to be deliberately distributed across the roadmap over time — giving each person a stream they drive end-to-end some of the time, and pairing or mobbing at other times, rather than picking one mode as “how we work now” and applying it uniformly until it stops working and gets swapped for its opposite.

Concretely, that might look like extending the same rotation instinct that shaping already applies to facilitation, into execution: an explicit “who owns driving this through to done” decision made once at the start of an initiative, visible to the team, changing initiative to initiative — rather than an implicit, unnamed scramble for it that shows up as either too many parallel streams or too many bored navigators, depending on which way the last retrospective nudged the dial.

That’s a bigger change than adjusting a WIP limit, and it’s probably a harder one to get agreement on — which is exactly why the team needs to see the diagnosis clearly first. Turning the dial feels like doing something. Naming what the dial can’t fix is less comfortable, but it’s the only version of this conversation that stops it from repeating next sprint.

Why driver rotation rarely survives contact with reality

There’s a smaller-scale version of the same tension worth naming, because it complicates the conclusion rather than just illustrating it. Pairing’s usual answer to navigator boredom is to rotate the driver frequently. In practice this rarely happens without real discipline enforcing it, and the reasons are structural, not laziness. Natural stopping points are genuinely hard to find mid-problem, and driving isn’t just operating the keyboard — it’s holding the shape of a half-built solution in your head. Handing that off mid-thought means someone else either preserves your intent or quietly replaces it with their own, and watching your own partially-built approach get overridden while you can only observe is a real discomfort, not reluctance to share credit. Rotate too eagerly and the rotation destroys the very ownership it was meant to produce.

There’s also a genuine efficiency case against it: “I could do this faster if I were just allowed to drive” is frequently true in the moment. What it discounts is that the return on letting someone else drive shows up later, as team capability, rather than now, as this task’s completion time — the same mistake as everything else here, just measuring the wrong feedback loop. Left unmanaged, the loop you can feel immediately wins by default over the one that actually matters. And sometimes there simply isn’t slack to spend on anyone’s growth right now, and some work is genuinely better driven by whoever’s fastest at it.

None of that undermines the point above. Rotation isn’t something that should happen mechanically, on principle, at every opportunity — it’s a deliberate default, consciously overridden when the cost of slowing down is genuinely too high, rather than something that silently never happens because nobody wanted to insist on it. That’s the actual difference between a team managing this tension on purpose and one stuck oscillating through it by accident.
