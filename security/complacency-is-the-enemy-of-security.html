Complacency is the Enemy of Security

Blog Logo

Tomas Basham

on 26 May 2026
About 4 min read

A particular kind of institutional blindness sets in when nothing has gone wrong for a while. It isn’t laziness, and it isn’t negligence in any obvious sense — it’s the slow, unannounced erosion of urgency that comes from an absence of consequences. Security has a name for this: complacency, and I think it’s more dangerous than most organisations are willing to admit, because it doesn’t look like a decision. It looks like the absence of one.

How it actually sets in

The organisations that suffer the worst security failures are rarely the ones that never thought about security. More often they’re the ones that thought about it once, set up reasonable foundations, shipped the product, watched it run without incident, and quietly stopped thinking about it. Over time, the absence of visible harm gets treated as evidence of safety, which it isn’t — it’s only evidence that harm hasn’t happened yet. The same pattern shows up well outside software: a driver who’s taken the same route for years without incident eases off the margins, a business that’s never had a fire stops testing the extinguishers.

What a disclosure is actually telling you

Security researchers find holes, report them through whatever disclosure process exists, and move on. Most engineering teams treat that report as a closed incident: fix shipped, researcher thanked, ticket closed. What gets missed is the more useful question underneath it — if someone with the skill and the inclination to look found this, what else is sitting there unfound? A researcher who discloses responsibly represents a narrow slice of the population capable of finding the same class of problem; treating their report as the whole story, rather than a sample of it, is usually where the complacency becomes visible from the outside.

What it looks like day to day

It rarely announces itself as a decision to deprioritise security. It looks like smaller, more reasonable-sounding things: a hardening task that keeps losing to something more urgent every sprint, a credential rotation that keeps getting deferred because coordinating it is annoying and nothing’s gone wrong yet, service-to-service trust that’s never been tightened because nobody’s had the time. None of this is unusual — most engineering organisations carry some version of it. The question worth asking isn’t whether that debt exists, but whether it’s being treated as inert or as compounding. Security debt compounds the same way any other technical debt does, except the interest is paid in an incident rather than in slower builds.

The asymmetry that makes this expensive to get wrong

Security has a structural asymmetry that complacent organisations tend not to sit with: you have to get it right every time, and an attacker only has to get it right once. Your organisation has a roadmap, competing priorities, and finite attention; an attacker has one objective and no sprint cycle to answer to. That asymmetry means the cost of complacency doesn’t accrue smoothly — it accumulates silently and then discharges all at once, at a time of the attacker’s choosing rather than yours. Reactive security work, done under that kind of pressure, costs considerably more than the same work done proactively — in engineering time, in trust, and sometimes in regulatory exposure.

This is also what makes security different from most other categories of product risk. Defer a performance fix and you see it in response times. Defer a UX fix and you see it in conversion. The feedback loop is tight enough to self-correct. Security’s feedback loop is either years long or instantaneous and severe, with very little in between — which means waiting for evidence that the risk is real is, in practice, waiting for the incident itself.

What the alternative actually looks like

The organisations with the strongest security posture aren’t the ones that recovered fastest from a breach — they’re the ones that treated the work as continuous and unglamorous, including during the quiet periods when nothing was obviously on fire. That’s a harder habit to sustain than it sounds, because there’s no natural internal pressure keeping it alive once the original incident that prompted the foundations fades from memory. It has to be maintained deliberately, as a standing property of how the organisation works, rather than revisited only when something forces the question.

The uncomfortable part is that most engineering leaders already know which items on their own backlog are this kind of risk — the credential hygiene that needs attention, the access that hasn’t been audited in a while. The honest question isn’t whether those things carry risk; everyone involved already knows they do. It’s whether the organisation treats security as something actively maintained, or as a state it’s passively in until proven otherwise.
